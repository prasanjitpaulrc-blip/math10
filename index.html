5. Check whether \((6^n)\) can end with the digit \(0\) for any natural number \(n\).

Solution:

A number ending in \(0\) must be divisible by \(10\).

    Since, \( 10=2\times5 \)

therefore the number must contain both factors \(2\) and \(5\).

Now,

\(\quad 6^{n}=(2\times3)^{n}=2^{n}\times3^{n} \)

This contains factors of \(2\) and \(3\) only and does not contain any factor of \(5\).

Therefore,

\(\quad 6^{n} \) cannot end with the digit \(0\) for any natural number \(n\).

Hence, the answer is No.

7. There is a circular path around a sports field. Sonia takes 18 minutes to drive one round of the field, while Ravi takes \(12\) minutes for the same. Suppose they both start at the same point and at the same time and go in the same direction. After how many minutes will they meet again at the starting point?

Solution: To meet again at the starting point, both must complete a whole number of rounds. Therefore, we find the LCM of \(18\) and \(12\).

\(\quad By \;prime \;factorisation\):

\(\qquad\quad 18=2\times3\times3 \)

\(\qquad\quad 12=2\times2\times3 \)

  \(Therefore\),

\(\quad LCM(18,12)=2\times2\times3\times3 \)

\(\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad =36 \)

Hence, Sonia and Ravi will meet again at the starting point after \(36\;minutes \)

8. \((i)\) The soldiers in a regiment can be stood in some rows consisting of \(15\), \(20\) or \(25\) numbers of soldiers. Find the least number of soldiers in the regiment.

Solution: The least number of soldiers must be exactly divisible by \(15,\;20\) and \(25\).

    Therefore, we find the LCM of \(15,\;20,\;25\).

\(\quad By \;prime \;factorisation\):

\(\quad\quad 15=3\times5 \)

\(\quad\quad 20=2\times2\times5 \)

\(\quad\quad 25=5\times5 \)

Taking the highest powers of all prime factors,

\(\quad LCM = 2^{2}\times3\times5^{2} \)

\(\quad\quad =4\times3\times25 \)

\(\quad\quad =300 \)

Therefore, the least number of soldiers in the regiment is \( 300 \)

\((ii)\) A bell rings at every \(18\) seconds, another bell rings at every \(60\) seconds. If these two bells ring simultaneously at an instant, then find after how many seconds will the bells ring simultaneously again.

Solution: The bells will ring together again after a time equal to the LCM of \(18\) and \(60\).

\(\quad By \;prime \;factorisation\):

\(\qquad\quad 18=2\times3\times3 \)

\(\qquad\quad 60=2\times2\times3\times5 \)

   Taking the highest powers of all prime factors,

\(\quad LCM = 2^{2}\times3^{2}\times5 \)

\(\quad\quad\quad =4\times9\times5 \)

\(\quad\quad\quad =180 \)

Therefore, both bells will ring simultaneously again after \( 180\;seconds\) \(or\) \(3\; minutes \)

1. Prove that \( \sqrt{5} \) is irrational.

Solution:Let us assume that \(\sqrt{5}\) is rational.

Then, there exist co-prime integers \(p\) and \(q\), such that

\( \qquad \sqrt{5}=\dfrac{p}{q} \),   where \(q\neq0\).

\( \quad\Rightarrow \sqrt{5}q=p \)

Squaring both sides.

\( \qquad (\sqrt{5}q)^{2}=p^{2} \)

\( \quad\Rightarrow\; 5q^{2}=p^{2}...... (i) \)

Since \(p^{2}\) is divisible by \(5\), \(p\) is also divisible by \(5\).


\( Let,\) \( \; p=5k \) for some integer \(k\).

\( \quad\Rightarrow p^2=(5k)^2 \)     [squaring both sides]

\( \quad\Rightarrow 5q^{2}=25k^{2} \)     [from eqn(i)]

\( \quad\Rightarrow q^{2}=5k^{2} \)

Since \(q^{2}\) is divisible by \(5\), \(q\) is also divisible by \(5\).


   This means both \(p\) and \(q\) are divisible by \(5\), which contradicts the fact that \(p\) and \(q\) are co-prime.

Hence, our assumption is false.


  \(Therefore, \;\sqrt5 \;is \;irrational\).

2. Prove that \( 3 + 2\sqrt{5} \) is irrational.

Solution: Let us assume that \( 3+2\sqrt{5} \) is rational.

Then, there exist co-prime integers \(p\) and \(q\), such that

\(\qquad\quad 3+2\sqrt{5}=\dfrac{p}{q} \) where \(q\ne0\).


\(\quad\quad \;\;\Rightarrow 2\sqrt{5}=\dfrac{p}{q}-3 \)


\(\quad\quad \;\;\Rightarrow \sqrt{5}=\dfrac{p-3q}{2q} \)


Since \( \dfrac{p-3q}{2q} \) is rational, \( \sqrt{5} \) becomes rational.

But we know that \( \sqrt{5} \) is irrational.

This is a contradiction.


\(Therefore, \; 3 + 2\sqrt5 \;is \;irrational.\)

\(Therefore,\;\dfrac1{\sqrt2}\;is\;irrational.\)



\((ii)\) Let us assume that \( 7\sqrt5 \) is rational.

\(\qquad\quad 7\sqrt5=\dfrac{p}{q} \) where \(q\ne0\).


\( \quad\quad \;\;\Rightarrow \sqrt5=\dfrac{p}{7q} \)


Since \( \dfrac{p}{7q} \) is rational, \( \sqrt5 \) becomes rational.

But we know that \( \sqrt5 \) is irrational.

This is a contradiction.


\(Therefore,\;7\sqrt5\;is\;irrational.\)



\((iii)\) Let us assume that \( 6+\sqrt2 \) is rational.

Then, there exist co-prime integers \(p\) and \(q\), such that

\(\qquad\quad 6+\sqrt2=\dfrac{p}{q} \) where \(q\ne0\).


\( \quad\quad \;\;\Rightarrow \sqrt2=\dfrac{p}{q}-6 \)


\( \quad\quad \;\;\Rightarrow \sqrt2=\dfrac{p-6q}{q} \)


Since \( \dfrac{p-6q}{q} \) is rational, \( \sqrt2 \) becomes rational.

But we know that \( \sqrt2 \) is irrational.

This is a contradiction.


\(Therefore,\;6+\sqrt2\;is\;irrational.\)

4. The product of a non-zero rational number and an irrational number is

  (A) always irrational
  (B) always rational
  (C) always Integer
  (D) rational or irrational

Solution: \( (A)\; always \;irrational \)


Explanation: Let a non-zero rational number be \( \frac{p}{q} \) and an irrational number be \( x \).

\(\quad\) Assume that their product is rational.

\(\quad\) \( \frac{p}{q}\times x=r \) where \(r\) is rational.

\(\quad\) Then, \( x=r\times\frac{q}{p} \)

\(\quad\) Since \( r,\frac{q}{p} \) are rational, their product is also rational.

\(\quad\) So, \( x \) becomes rational, which is impossible because \(x\) is irrational.

\(\quad\) Hence, our assumption is wrong.

\(\;\;\) Therefore, the product of a non-zero rational number and an irrational number is always irrational.

\( \sqrt2+\sqrt5 \) is a special case of \( \sqrt p+\sqrt q \).

Therefore the reason directly explains the assertion.

7. \(Assertion (A)\) : \( \sqrt{a}\) is an irrational number, when \(a\) is a prime number.
   \(Reason (R)\) : Square root of any prime number is an irrational number.

\(\quad \)\((A)\) Both \((A)\) and \((R)\) are true and \(Reason (R)\) is the correct explanation of \(Assertion (A)\)
\(\quad \) \((B)\) \((A)\) and \((R)\) are true but \(Reason (R)\) is not the correct explanation of \(Assertion (A)\)
\(\quad \) \((C)\) \(Assertion (A)\) is true but \(Reason (R)\) is false
\(\quad \) \((D)\) \((A)\) \(Assertion (A)\) is false but \(Reason (R)\) is true

Solution:\( (A)\) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)


Explanation:

Assertion (A): If \(a\) is a prime number, then \( \sqrt a \) is irrational.

Reason (R): Square root of any prime number is irrational.

The reason directly states why the assertion is true.

Therefore, both Assertion and Reason are true and Reason correctly explains Assertion.

8.   \( \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}, \sqrt{6}, \sqrt{7},\sqrt{8},\sqrt{10}\) are all irrationals
Which pair among them is like irrational?

(A) \( \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{6} \)
(B) \( \sqrt{8}, \sqrt{10} \)
(C) \( \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{8} \)
(D) \( \sqrt{7}, \sqrt{8} \)

Solution: \( (C)\; \sqrt2,\sqrt8 \)


Explanation:Like irrationals are irrational numbers whose irrational parts are the same.

We simplify the given options:

(A) \( \sqrt6 \ne k\sqrt3 \) Not like irrationals.

(B) \( \sqrt8=2\sqrt2 \) but \( \sqrt{10} \) cannot be simplified. Not like irrationals.

(C) \( \sqrt8=\sqrt{4\times2}=2\sqrt2 \) So, \( \sqrt2,\;2\sqrt2 \) have the same irrational part. Hence they are like irrationals.

(D) \( \sqrt8=2\sqrt2 \) which is not of the form \(k\sqrt7\). So not like irrationals.

NCERT Class 10 Math Chapter 1 – Real Numbers FAQs

Clear conceptual answers to the most common queries from Real Numbers.

What is Euclid's Division Lemma?

For any two positive integers a and b, there exist unique integers q and r such that \( a = bq + r \) , where \( 0 \le r < b \). It is used to compute HCF.

What is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic?

Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order of the prime factors.

How to prove \( \sqrt{2} \) is irrational?

Assume \( \sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q} \) in lowest terms. Then \( 2 = p^2/q^2 \) → \( p^2 = 2q^2 \). So p is even, then q is even, contradicting coprimality. Therefore \( \sqrt{2} \) is irrational.

When does a rational number have a terminating decimal expansion?

A rational number \( \frac{p}{q} \) (in simplest form) has a terminating decimal expansion if and only if the denominator q has only 2 and 5 as prime factors.

What is the relationship between HCF and LCM?

For any two positive integers a and b, HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a × b.

Can a rational number be non‑terminating non‑repeating?

No, rational numbers always have either terminating or non‑terminating repeating decimal expansions. Non‑terminating non‑repeating decimals are irrational.

How do you find LCM using prime factorisation?

LCM = product of the greatest powers of all prime factors appearing in the numbers.

Why is 7 × 11 × 13 + 13 composite?

Factorise: 13(7×11 + 1) = 13 × 78, which has factors other than 1 and itself, so it is composite.

What is the HCF of two consecutive even numbers?

HCF of two consecutive even numbers is 2. Example: HCF(8,10) = 2.

Can a number end with 0 if its prime factorisation lacks 5?

No. For a number to end with 0, it must have both 2 and 5 in its prime factorisation.
